## 1. What a $15 price says

When the host travels, he skips the cheapest barber on purpose. He doesn’t debate it. He sees $15 and moves on. Before the cape goes on, the client already has an opinion, and the price is a big part of that opinion. A cheap number says things the barber may not mean to say: still learning, will take anybody, will rush you. You might be the best barber in the city. A $15 price tells people you’re not.

> “You might be the best barber in the city, but a $15 price is telling people you’re not.”  
> — Clippers and Shears, Episode 1

## 2. Why the number feels so hard to change

Raising a price is “just a number” on the board, and it can still feel like jumping off a roof. The episode traces that feeling to how a lot of people were raised: talking about money is rude, charge what people can afford, be grateful for the clients you have, and if you’re too expensive they’ll go down the street. Being cheap gets mistaken for being humble, or for being nice.

> “Being cheap is being tired.”  
> — Clippers and Shears, Episode 1

## 3. “You’re way too cheap” is not a compliment

A regular of about two years loves the cut, hands over $35, and says the barber is way too cheap. The barber says thank you, as if it were a compliment. It wasn’t. That moment is why they made the episode.

> “It was not a compliment.”  
> — Clippers and Shears, Episode 1

## 4. Raise the service before the price

You can’t just change the number and deliver the same haircut. If the cut goes from $35 to $55 and the client gets last week’s exact service, “he’s gonna feel some kind of way.” The rule in the episode is to raise the service first, so the price matches the promise: a clean station, tools that look cared for, being on time, a real consultation before the clippers, and small extras that don’t cost much.

> “Before you raise your price, you raise your service.”  
> — Clippers and Shears, Episode 1

## 5. How to tell clients the price is going up

The host’s first attempt started with an apology. The correction in the episode is blunt: you are not begging for more money. You are informing clients about a business decision. Groceries don’t apologise when eggs go up. The three steps: give two to four weeks’ notice, be clear and don’t ramble, and thank them for their loyalty (warm, not cold), then stop talking.

> “Listen, you are not begging for more money. You’re informing your clients about a business decision.”  
> — Clippers and Shears, Episode 1

Where to post the notice, and how to reward the clients who’ve been with you longest: [How to Raise Your Prices Without Losing Your Best Clients](/blog/raise-your-prices-without-losing-clients).

## 6. Do your floor math tonight

If a listener does only one thing this week, the episode’s answer is to do the floor math tonight. Write down monthly business costs, write down what you want to take home, add them, divide by the days you want to work, then divide by how many clients you comfortably see in a day. That number is the floor. If the current price is already under it, the question is not whether to raise. It’s when.

> “And that number is your floor.”  
> — Clippers and Shears, Episode 1

## FAQ

**Why does raising a barber’s price feel so hard?**  
Because a lot of people were raised to treat talk about money as rude, and to “charge what people can afford.” The episode says that feeling is why a simple change on the board can feel like jumping off a roof.

**What does a cheap price say to a client?**  
It can say “I’m still learning,” “I’ll take anybody,” and “I’m going to rush you,” even when none of that is true.

**What if everybody leaves after a price increase?**  
The episode’s honest answer: some people will leave, not everybody. A price increase works like a filter. The people who trust you tend to stay; the people who were only there for the price are more likely to go.

**What is the one thing to do tonight?**  
Sit down with a piece of paper or the notes app and work out the floor: costs plus take-home, divided by working days, divided by comfortable clients per day.
