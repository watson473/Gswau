## 1. Do your floor math tonight

If a listener does only one thing this week, the episode’s answer is to do the floor math tonight. That number is where pricing a haircut, a fade, or any other service in the chair starts. It isn’t a price list for a city or a shop. It’s the floor under *your* chair.

> “Do your floor math tonight.”  
> — Clippers and Shears, Episode 1

## 2. How to work out the floor

Sit down with a piece of paper or the notes app. Write down monthly business costs, write down what you want to take home, add them, divide by the days you want to work, then divide by how many clients you comfortably see in a day. That number is the floor. If what you’re charging right now is already under it, you’re not deciding whether to raise. You’re deciding when.

Not sure what counts as a monthly business cost? The list in [Know Your Numbers](/blog/know-your-numbers-real-cost-of-a-haircut) covers it: booth rent or your share of shop costs, products and supplies, tools, booking software, phone and card fees, insurance, licences and education. How you work changes which of those you carry. [Booth Rent or Commission: Which Pays You More?](/blog/booth-rent-or-commission) shows how each setup moves the costs onto you or onto the shop.

A fade doesn’t get a different formula. The floor still comes from costs, take-home, working days and the clients you can comfortably see. This page won’t guess how long a fade takes or what one should cost.

> “And that number is your floor.”  
> — Clippers and Shears, Episode 1

## 3. Name the problem before you say the number

The floor tells you what the number has to cover. It doesn’t tell you how to *say* the number. The short lesson is about the order of the consult: don’t lead with the price. You said the number before you named what was bothering them. They didn’t come in for a price. They came in with a problem. Lead with the number, and that’s all they hear.

The first two steps in the lesson: ask what’s bothering them (“What’s not working with your hair right now?”), then say it back. The example in the video: “So it puffs out on the sides, and grows out too fast?”

> “They didn’t come in for a price. They came in with a problem.”  
> — GSWAU short lesson, “Price Before The Problem”

## 4. The price can stay the same and still land as the fix

Here’s the twist in that lesson: the price doesn’t change. But once the problem is named, the number sounds like the fix. Then, and only then, give the number, tied to that fix. Problem first. The number can wait.

> “The price doesn’t change. But once the problem is named, the number sounds like the fix.”  
> — GSWAU short lesson, “Price Before The Problem”

Want the whole consult, from hello to the yes before you cut? See [How to Talk to Barber Clients](/blog/how-to-talk-to-barber-clients).

## 5. A busy week is a feeling. Three numbers tell you more

A busy week is a feeling. Three numbers tell you what’s really going on. Go by feel, and a packed day can hide a slow week.

The three numbers in the lesson:

1. Count every client you cut each week.
2. Track rebooks: who books their next visit before they leave.
3. Know your average ticket: what you took in, divided by clients served.

Those are the numbers that sit behind the floor, and behind whether a haircut or fade price is actually working for you.

> “A busy week is a feeling. Three numbers tell you what’s really going on.”  
> — GSWAU short lesson, “Know Your Numbers”

## 6. Pick one number to grow this month

Don’t chase all three. Pick one number to grow this month. The lesson gives two ways to do it: ask for the rebook, or add a beard detail. Then check again. Count it. Pick one. Grow it.

If rebooks are the number you pick, [Rebooking at the Chair: Fill Next Month Before Today Ends](/blog/rebook-at-the-chair) shows how to ask for the rebook.

> “Don’t chase all three. Pick one number to grow this month.”  
> — GSWAU short lesson, “Know Your Numbers”

## FAQ

**How do I price a haircut or a fade?**  
Work out your floor first: monthly business costs plus what you want to take home, divided by the days you want to work, divided by how many clients you comfortably see in a day. The same floor math applies to a fade as to any other cut. This page doesn’t give a local price.

**What if my current price is already under the floor?**  
Then you already have your answer. You’re not deciding whether to raise. You’re deciding when.

When it’s time, start with the mindset in [Your Price Is a Message](/blog/clippers-and-shears-ep-1-your-price-is-a-message) (Clippers and Shears, Episode 1), then follow the step-by-step plan in [How to Raise Your Prices Without Losing Your Best Clients](/blog/raise-your-prices-without-losing-clients).

**Should I say the price first?**  
No. Name what’s bothering them first, say it back, then give the number tied to that fix. Lead with the number, and that’s all they hear.

**Does the price have to change for it to feel like the fix?**  
In this lesson, the price doesn’t change. Once the problem is named, the same number can sound like the fix.

**What numbers should I actually track?**  
Clients you cut each week, rebooks before they leave, and average ticket (what you took in, divided by clients served). Pick one to grow this month.
